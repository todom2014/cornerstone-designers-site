Do accessibility widgets make your website ADA compliant?

No. The FTC fined the best-known widget maker $1 million over that exact claim. Here's what the widgets do, what the lawsuits show, and what actually works.

By Taylor Odom  ·  September 26, 2026

The short answer: no. A widget that adds a toolbar to your site doesn't fix the code underneath, and the Federal Trade Commission fined the best-known widget company $1 million in 2025 for claiming its product made websites compliant.

If you run a business website, you've probably been offered one: a small icon in the corner that promises to make your site "ADA compliant" for a monthly fee, with no rebuilding. It's an easy sell, because the alternative sounds expensive. Here's why it doesn't do what it says, and what to do instead.

What an accessibility widget actually does

Accessibility widgets, also called overlays, are a line of JavaScript added to a site. They usually add a menu with options like bigger text, higher contrast or a reading guide, and some claim to use AI to repair problems automatically.

The trouble is where the problems live. A screen reader can't tell what an unlabeled form field is for. A keyboard user gets stuck in a menu that only works with a mouse. A photo has no text description. These are problems in the site's code, and a toolbar sitting on top of the page doesn't rewrite that code reliably. Many people who rely on assistive technology already have their own tools for text size and contrast, built into their phone or computer.

What the FTC found

In January 2025 the Federal Trade Commission announced an order against accessiBe, one of the largest widget sellers, and finalized it on April 22, 2025. The FTC said:

  • accessiBe marketed its accessWidget as able to make any website compliant with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), and the tool did not make all sites compliant.
  • The company formatted third-party articles and reviews to look like independent opinions without disclosing its connection to them.

accessiBe was ordered to pay $1 million and is barred from claiming that an automated product can make a website WCAG-compliant unless it has evidence to back that up.

Are small businesses really getting sued?

Yes, and in large numbers.

  • 8,667 ADA Title III lawsuits were filed in federal court in 2025, according to the law firm Seyfarth Shaw. That counts every kind of Title III case, including buildings and parking lots, not only websites. It was down 2% from 2024, and still about three times the 2013 level.
  • UsableNet, which tracks website and app cases in federal and state courts, counted more than 5,000 digital accessibility lawsuits in 2025.
  • In UsableNet's data, 45% of federal filings targeted companies that had been sued before.
  • Sites using accessibility widgets kept getting sued all year, with more than 150 cases in a single month at the peak. The complaints often pointed to barriers still sitting in the code.
  • E-commerce made up about 70% of the website cases and food service about 21%.

Most cases are filed in New York, Florida and California, but a website can be reached from anywhere.

What actually makes a website accessible

The standard courts and regulators point to is WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. Level AA of WCAG 2.1 is the usual target. In plain terms, it means things like:

  • Every image that carries meaning has a text description.
  • Every form field has a label that a screen reader announces.
  • Everything works with a keyboard, with a visible outline showing where you are.
  • Text has enough contrast against its background to read.
  • Headings are real headings, in order, so people can skim by structure.
  • Nothing depends on color alone to carry meaning.
  • Videos have captions.

These are fixed in the site itself, while it's being built or rebuilt. That's the only version that holds up.

How to check your own website in ten minutes

You don't need special software to spot the biggest problems:

  1. Put your mouse away. Press Tab to move through your site. Can you reach every link, button and form field? Can you always see where you are?
  2. Zoom to 200%. In your browser, press Ctrl or Cmd with + until it reads 200%. Does anything overlap, disappear or need sideways scrolling?
  3. Try your contact form without looking at the labels. If a field's only label is grey placeholder text that vanishes when you type, that's a problem.
  4. Run a free checker. WebAIM's WAVE tool (wave.webaim.org) flags missing labels, missing image descriptions and contrast errors. Automated tools only catch part of what matters, but they catch the obvious part.

Frequently asked questions

Does the ADA apply to my business website?

The Department of Justice's 2022 guidance says the ADA's rules for businesses open to the public cover the goods and services they offer on the web. It also says there's no DOJ regulation setting detailed technical standards for business websites, and it points to WCAG as helpful guidance.

Is WCAG 2.1 AA the law?

Not for private businesses: there's no codified technical standard for them. It's the benchmark most often used in practice. (For state and local governments, a 2024 federal rule does adopt WCAG 2.1 AA.)

Can I remove my widget right away?

Removing it won't make anything worse on its own, since the code problems were there anyway. The fix is repairing the site itself. Start with the ten-minute check above.

Will fixing my site stop me being sued?

Nobody can promise that. It does fix the barriers that lawsuits point to, and it makes your site usable by more of your customers, which is the point.
