How much does a small business website cost in South Carolina? (2026)

Real 2026 price ranges for a small business website, from DIY builders to agencies, the yearly costs nobody quotes you, and our own published prices.

The short answer: most small businesses should budget $3,000 to $12,000 for a professionally built website, plus a small yearly amount for the domain and hosting. Under that, you're usually building it yourself. Over it, you're paying for a large agency, an online store, or custom features.

That range comes from 2026 national pricing guides. We couldn't find a reliable South Carolina-only survey, but the same three things set the price here as anywhere: who builds the site, how many pages it has, and what it has to do.

Below: what each option really costs, what pushes the price up, the costs that arrive after launch, and what we charge.

What each option actually costs

A 2026 pricing guide from Pitchsite puts the typical small business website at $3,000–$12,000 from a freelancer or small agency, and breaks it down by who does the work:

Who builds it Typical price
You, on a builder like Squarespace or Wix $20–$50 a month
A junior freelancer $1,500–$4,000
An experienced freelancer $4,000–$12,000
A small studio (1–10 people) $6,000–$20,000
A mid-size agency (10–50 people) $15,000–$75,000

Elementor's 2026 breakdown lands in the same place: $2,000–$8,000 for a simple 5–10 page site from a freelancer, $10,000–$35,000 for a small business site from an agency, and $20,000–$55,000+ for a custom online store.

Hourly rates explain most of the spread. Pitchsite lists $30–$60 an hour for junior freelancers, $75–$150 for experienced ones, and $100–$250 blended across an agency team. A site that takes 60 hours costs very different amounts depending on whose hours they are.

What pushes the price up

Two sites with the same number of pages can be quoted thousands apart. These are the usual reasons:

  • Number of pages. A four-page site (home, services, about, contact) is a different job from one with a page for every service, location and team member.
  • Selling online. Products, checkout, shipping and tax rules add real work, and a large catalog adds more.
  • Booking and integrations. Connecting a scheduling tool, a CRM, or payment system takes setup and testing.
  • Words and photos. If the designer writes your copy or arranges photography, that's more hours than dropping in text you supply.
  • Accessibility done properly. Building to WCAG 2.1 AA in the code takes care, and it's worth paying for. (A widget bolted on afterwards is not the same thing; we wrote about why accessibility overlays don't make a site compliant.)
  • Speed. A rush deadline usually costs extra, because something else gets moved.

The costs after launch

The build is the big number, but it isn't the only one. Elementor's figures for the running costs:

  • Domain name: about $10–$20 a year.
  • Hosting: $10–$50 a month for WordPress hosting. Simple static sites can often be hosted for little or nothing.
  • Maintenance plans: $50–$200 a month, if you want someone else handling updates and small edits.
  • SEO retainers: $500–$2,500 or more a month, which is a separate service from building the site.

None of these are mandatory except the domain. Ask any designer which ones their site needs to keep working. If the answer is "our monthly plan, or it stops," you don't really own the site.

Why so many web designers won't tell you the price

Ask most studios what a website costs and you'll get "it depends," followed by a calendar link. Sometimes that's genuine. Often it's a way of finding out what you can afford before quoting.

A price you can't see is a price that can move. If two businesses ask for the same site and one of them sounds bigger on the call, they may not be quoted the same number. Published prices take that off the table, and they save everyone a meeting when the budget doesn't fit.

How to budget for your website

  1. Decide what the site has to do. Get calls? Take bookings? Sell products? That one answer decides most of the price.
  2. Count the pages you actually need. List your services. If each one deserves its own page for Google, you're past a starter site.
  3. Check who's writing the words. If it's you, set aside the time. If it's the designer, expect it in the quote.
  4. Add the first year of running costs. Domain and hosting at minimum; support only if you want it.
  5. Get the price in writing, with the scope beside it. A number without a list of what it covers isn't a price yet.

Frequently asked questions

Is a $500 website worth it?

It can be, for a business that just needs to exist online. At that price you're usually getting a template with your details in it, and you should check who owns the domain and whether the site keeps working if you stop paying.

Do I have to pay every month for a website?

Only for the domain (yearly) and some form of hosting. Maintenance and SEO plans are optional services. With us, monthly support is optional and has no effect on who owns the site.

Is it cheaper to pay a designer by the hour?

Sometimes, but you carry the risk if it takes longer. A fixed price moves that risk to the designer. That's why we don't bill hourly.

How long does it take to build a small business website?

Our Starter Site takes 1–2 weeks and our Business Site 3–4 weeks. Most delays come from waiting on content, so we ask for everything in one checklist at the start.
